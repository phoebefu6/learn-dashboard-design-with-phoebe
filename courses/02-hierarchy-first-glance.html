Learn Dashboard Design with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

Hierarchy and the first glance

Session 1 ended with a sentence Nia should be able to say thirty seconds after the screen opens. Tonight is about where her eyes go in those thirty seconds, because they go somewhere whether or not the designer chose it. Every tile on a screen has a weight, made of its size, its contrast against the ground and its position, and the heaviest tile is read first no matter what the title says. We take that weight apart, use it to set a reading order on purpose, pick the one number that leads, and learn the squint test that shows the order a layout really has. Along the way we read two famous claims at source, the F-pattern and Miller's seven, and find that neither says what dashboards are usually told it says.

Part 1 · covers visual weight: size x contrast x position

What the eye lands on, and why

5 min live

Open any screen and your eyes land somewhere before you have decided anything. What they land on is the element with the most visual weight, and weight is not mysterious: it is the product of how big a thing is, how strongly it contrasts with what surrounds it, and where it sits on the page. Three dials, one product. A designer who leaves the dials on their defaults has still set them, and the tool's defaults make every tile the same weight, which means the eye picks at random and the glance is spent finding instead of reading.

LiveThree dials, one product3 min▶

Size is the dial everyone knows. A bigger number is read before a smaller one, and a tile twice the area of its neighbours is read first unless the other two dials are turned against it. Contrast is the dial most tools quietly disable: when every tile is a white card on a white page with the same grey label, nothing stands out from anything, and a bold value in dark ink on a light ground is the cheapest way to turn it back up. Position is the dial that works without any ink at all. In a left-to-right reading culture the top-left of a screen is read first and the bottom-right last, and the tool's grid gives the top-left slot to whichever tile was created first, which is rarely the one that should lead.

The word that matters is product. A tile that is big but pale and bottom-right loses to a tile that is medium, dark and top-left. At Saltmarsh (constructed) Tomas's first draft had six identical cards in creation order, so the tile that led was "carrier share of late orders", the last one anyone needed on Monday. Nothing was wrong with the data. The dials were on their defaults.

Self-studyThe one number, and the tiles that compete with it3 min▶

Every dashboard has one number that is read first, and the honest question is whether it is the right one. Nia's sentence from session 1 starts with deliveries, because the carrier call is the decision with the shortest fuse, so the one number is the late share of deliveries this week. On the figure it carries Olist's median late share of 4.9 percent, named as Olist's, because no Saltmarsh number is a measurement. Once the one number is chosen, every other tile is scored against it: nothing else on the screen may be bigger, darker or further up-left than the one number, or the layout is arguing with the brief.

Two tiles will try to compete. The first is the one the sponsor likes, which at Saltmarsh (constructed) is revenue, because Priya asked for the dashboard and reads it on Monday evening. It stays on the screen, in the second row, at the second weight, and the reason is said out loud: the money question decides whether to act at all, but it is asked after the deliveries question, not before. The second competitor is the prettiest chart, which is usually the delivery days trend, and a pretty chart with a low weight is a fine thing. Charts themselves, which chart for which data and how to draw the marks, belong to Dataviz: https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-dataviz-with-phoebe/ . Tonight is only about how much of the glance each one is allowed to take.

Real world

"Why does everyone look at the wrong number?" A familiar pattern: a leader opens the weekly screen, reads the first big figure out loud, and the team spends five minutes on it before someone points out that the figure that mattered is lower down. The leader was not careless. Their eyes went to the heaviest tile, which was the one the tool put top-left and the designer never moved. The fix costs nothing: swap two tiles and make the one that matters a size larger. The meeting changes the following week.

Part 2 · covers reading order, and the F-pattern read at source

Reading order is yours to set

5 min live

Weight decides where the eye lands first. The layout then decides where it goes next, and next, and next, and that route is the reading order. Nia's sentence has four slots in a fixed order: deliveries, demand, money, backlog. The screen should walk her eyes through the four in that order without her having to search, so the route is drawn as numbered stops before any tile is placed. This is also where the most quoted "law" of web layout gets read at source, because the F-pattern is not the route a dashboard should impose; it is the route eyes take when a page has imposed none.

LiveWhat NN/g actually measured, and what it called it3 min▶

In 2006 Jakob Nielsen published an eyetracking study at the Nielsen Norman Group: 232 users, thousands of web pages, and a dominant pattern with three parts. A horizontal movement across the upper part of the content, a shorter second horizontal movement lower down, and a vertical scan down the left side. The shape is an F. The page's own conclusions are about writing, not layout: "Users won't read your text thoroughly", "The first two paragraphs must state the most important information", and "Start subheads, paragraphs, and bullet points with information-carrying words".

In 2017 Kara Pernice went back to it on the same site and named the conditions under which the F appears: a page with little or no formatting, a wall of text; a user trying to be efficient; and a user not committed to reading every word. Her verdict is verbatim and unambiguous: "the F-shaped scanning pattern is bad for users and businesses". The fix she prescribes is formatting that directs attention: headings, bullets, bold, front-loaded words, and cutting content. So the sentence "users read in an F, so put the important thing top-left" has a true half and a false half. The eye does start top-left. But the F is what eyes do when a page gives them nothing to hold, and a dashboard has almost no running text, so there is nothing for an F to form on. The usable lesson is the one NN/g draws: put the most important thing first, and make the structure visible so no scanning pattern is needed at all.

Self-studyDrawing the route so no F is needed3 min▶

Tomas writes the route as four numbered stops taken straight from Nia's sentence, then places tiles so that weight falls in that order: stop 1 is the biggest and darkest tile, top-left; stops 2 and 3 are the next weight, to its right, one above the other so the eye drops rather than hunts; stop 4 is the bottom row, which at Saltmarsh (constructed) is the backlog by stage, Kofi's question, asked last because he answers it himself. The three small tiles along the bottom share the lowest weight because none of them is a question; they are the context a question's answer needs, and context is read only if the answer surprises.

Check the route with one question per arrow: when the eye finishes stop 1, is stop 2 the next heaviest thing in its field of view, and is nothing heavier in the way? A route breaks when a stop-4 tile is given a bright chart, because the chart outweighs the stop-2 number and the eye jumps the queue. The repair is never to brighten stop 2 as well; it is to quieten stop 4. Weight is relative, and turning everything up returns the screen to the tool's default, where every tile weighs the same and the glance is spent searching. How a leader reads such a screen once it is built is the Business Intelligence course's page: https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-business-intelligence-with-phoebe/courses/a2-reading-a-dashboard.html .

Read the route out loud with a finger. Open the draft, say "one, two, three, four" and touch each tile in the order the sentence needs. If your finger has to cross back over a tile it already passed, or hovers while you look for the next stop, the layout has imposed a different order from the one you wrote, and the dials of the tile you hovered near need turning down.

Part 3 · covers the squint test, and Miller 1956 read for what it does not claim

The squint test, and the seven that was never about screens

5 min live

You cannot see your own layout's weight once you know what every tile says, because knowing where the number is feels the same as seeing it first. The squint test takes the knowing away. Blur the screen, by squinting, by stepping back three metres from the projector, or by shrinking the window until the labels are unreadable, and what remains is the layout's real hierarchy: blocks of weight with no words. If the blocks do not rank in the order of the route, the route is a wish. This is also the Part where the other famous number gets read at source, because "no more than seven tiles, Miller said so" is quoted in every dashboard review and the paper says no such thing.

LiveSquint, or step back three metres3 min▶

The test takes ten seconds and needs no tool. Put the draft on the meeting-room screen, stand at the back wall, half-close your eyes, and say which block you see first, second and third. Then compare that to the route. At Saltmarsh (constructed) Tomas's second draft failed it: the delivery days trend chart, a bright line on a white card, read as the first block from the back of the room, ahead of the late deliveries number it was meant to support. The chart was not wrong. It was too heavy for stop 4, so it got a lighter line and a smaller card, and the one number came back to the front.

The squint test also catches the opposite fault, which is a screen with no hierarchy at all: from three metres it is a grid of identical grey blocks, and that means the dials are on their defaults. Vision science reports that a handful of attributes, hue, size, orientation, position and intensity, are picked up in a glance before focused attention, while a conjunction of two of them is not (Healey and Enns 2012 and Ware 2012, reported; no millisecond figure is quoted because the primary pages were not read at source). Weight works because it is built from exactly those attributes: size and intensity and position are the three dials. A layout that depends on reading a label to find the lead has asked for focused attention before the glance has started.

Self-studyMiller 1956, read: a span of judgment, not a tile count3 min▶

George Miller's 1956 paper in Psychological Review, "The magical number seven, plus or minus two", is read here in full. The seven is two things. It is the span of absolute judgment for one-dimensional stimuli: how many loudnesses, pitches or line lengths people can tell apart without error, with channel capacities around 2.6 bits, about 6.5 categories. And it is the span of immediate memory, counted in chunks, where "recoding" lets one chunk carry more. The paper's visual-display measurements are laboratory judgments of line length and position. Kaufman and colleagues' dot patterns are in there too: no errors up to about five or six dots, which is subitizing, with Miller's own caution that the link to seven may be coincidence. His closing words are verbatim: "I suspect that it is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence."

The paper says nothing about screens, displays of information, or how many items belong on one. "No more than seven tiles" is an extrapolation Miller did not make, and quoting him for it is quoting a paper for a claim it does not contain. What the paper does support is the chunking move: if the reader holds things in chunks, group the tiles so there are four groups to hold, not nineteen tiles. That is why the squinted panel in the figure shows one block and two groups rather than six shapes, and why session 3 spends its time on grouping by the question rather than counting tiles. Miller also notes that adding dimensions raises total capacity but lowers accuracy per attribute, which is a fair warning against a tile that encodes three things at once.

Real world

"Miller says seven, so cut it to seven." A familiar pattern in design reviews: a screen with nineteen tiles is sent back with "Miller's law" in the comment, the team deletes twelve tiles, and the seven that remain are still seven unranked blocks the eye picks between at random. The number was never the problem. A screen of seven tiles with no weight and no groups fails the squint test exactly as a screen of nineteen does. Rank them, group them, and the count takes care of itself.

Build-along

Tomas's first-glance layout toy

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas builds the layout on paper before he opens a tool, with a scoring sheet that is constructed for teaching and says so: each tile gets a 1-to-3 score on each dial, the three scores multiply into a weight, the weights rank the tiles, and the rank places them on the screen. Then the draft is squinted at and the route is read out loud. Do the same for the six tiles of your own brief from session 1; if you have none in hand, use Saltmarsh (constructed). The scores are judgments, not measurements, and the point of writing them down is to make the judgments arguable.

Copy the route from the sentence. Line 4 of the brief already has the order. Write it as numbered stops, one per question, and give every tile on the screen a stop or the word "context". A tile with neither is a session 1 problem: it has no decision and should not be here.

★ Step 1 · the route, from Nia's sentenceStop 1 deliveries fine / slipping -> late deliveries this week Stop 2 demand up / down -> orders this week Stop 3 money up / down -> revenue this week Stop 4 backlog in picking / packing -> backlog by stage context -> delivery days vs promise context -> carrier share of late orders Saltmarsh, constructed. Six tiles, four stops, two pieces of context.

Score the three dials on each tile. One to three on size, contrast and position, where 3 means "the most this screen will give to anything". The scale is a constructed teaching device, so say so when you use it, and argue about each score out loud: the argument is the design.

★ Step 2 · the scoring sheet (scale constructed for teaching)Tile Size Contrast Position Weight late deliveries 3 3 3 27 orders this week 2 2 3 12 revenue this week 2 2 2 8 backlog by stage 1 2 3 6 delivery days 1 2 1 2 carrier share 1 1 1 1 Rule: no tile may out-score the stop-1 tile on any single dial.

Rank, and check the rank against the route. The weights should fall in stop order. Where they do not, the layout is arguing with the brief, and the fix is to turn a dial down on the tile that jumped the queue, never to turn everything else up.

★ Step 3 · rank against routeRank by weight: late deliveries 27 > orders 12 > revenue 8 > backlog 6 > days 2 > carrier 1 Route order: stop 1 deliveries, stop 2 demand, stop 3 money, stop 4 backlog, then context Match: yes. First draft did not: delivery days had a bright line chart, contrast 3, weight 1 x 3 x 1 = 3 and it read before backlog from the back of the room. Line quietened: contrast 2, weight 2.

Place by rank on a grid. Heaviest top-left, then to its right, then down. Keep the stop-1 tile at least twice the area of any other and give it the darkest value on the screen. The number on the tile is Olist's, and the tile says so, because Saltmarsh measures nothing.

Run the squint test on the draft. Stand at the back of the room, half-close your eyes, and name the blocks in the order they appear. Write the order down before you compare it to the route, so the comparison is honest.

★ Step 5 · squint logSeen first: one large block, top-left = stop 1 ok Seen second: a pair of slim blocks, right of it = stops 2, 3 ok Seen third: a row of three low blocks = stop 4 + context ok Seen at all? the trend line in delivery days = no, good Verdict: blocks rank in route order. Draft passes.

Read the route out loud with a finger, timed. Say Nia's sentence while touching stops 1 to 4. If the finger crosses back or pauses to search, note where, and turn down the dial of the tile it paused near. The time is Saltmarsh's and constructed; yours is the one to measure, and session 5 measures it with five readers.

★ Step 6 · the read-back"Deliveries are fine at 4.9% late (Olist's median), demand is up on last week, money is up, and the backlog is in packing." Finger route: 1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 4, no crossing back, no pause. Time: under the 30 s glance from session 1 (constructed; re-measure in session 5).

Keep the ledger next to the draft. When a stakeholder asks for their tile to be "a bit more prominent", put the ledger in front of them and ask which dial, by how much, and which tile it overtakes. Most requests end at "one step up on size, still below stop 1", which the layout can afford. The ledger turns a taste argument into an arithmetic one.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Score a screen you did not build. Take any dashboard your team uses and fill the scoring sheet for its tiles. Rank by weight and write down which tile leads. Then ask the person who opens it which number they read first. Note whether the two agree.
  2. Squint at your own draft. Put the layout from tonight's build-along on a screen, step back three metres, and log the blocks in the order they appear. Compare to your route. Fix one dial, not all of them.
  3. Find one F. Look for a page in your organisation, a wiki page or a long report, where your own eyes make the F that NN/g describes. Say which of Pernice's three conditions it meets, and what one formatting change would remove the need for the F.
  4. Count your groups, not your tiles. On your draft, draw a line around every set of tiles that answers one question. Bring the number of groups; session 3 starts from it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A revenue tile is the largest on the screen, but it is a pale grey chart in the bottom-right corner. The late deliveries tile is half the size, dark ink on a light card, top-left. Which is read first, and why?

Weight multiplies. A big tile with low contrast in the lowest-weight position loses to a medium tile with high contrast top-left. The sponsor's preference is a reason to put a tile on the screen, not a reason for it to lead; the route from the brief decides that, and no study is needed to apply the three dials.

2 · Tomas's draft passes the scoring sheet, but from the back of the room the first block anyone sees is the delivery days chart. What has happened, and what is the fix?

The squint test is the check on the ledger, not the other way round. When they disagree, a score was wrong, almost always the contrast of a chart. Turning the lead tile up as well returns the screen towards the default where everything weighs the same. Miller's paper makes no recommendation about tile counts.

3 · A reviewer writes "users read in an F pattern, so follow the F when laying out the dashboard". What did NN/g actually find, as read?

Nielsen 2006 measured the F on text-heavy web pages; Pernice 2017 named the three conditions under which it appears and called the pattern "bad for users and businesses". A dashboard has almost no running text, so the usable lesson is NN/g's own: put the most important thing first and make the structure visible. The pattern is real and still relevant on walls of text; it is not a layout law.

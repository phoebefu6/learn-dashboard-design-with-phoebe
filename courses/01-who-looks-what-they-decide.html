Learn Dashboard Design with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Who looks, and what they decide

Most dashboards begin with a data source and a tool, and end as a wall of everything the source can show. The good ones begin with a person who opens one screen on a schedule, a question that person is already asking, and a decision the answer changes. Tonight we take Stephen Few's definition apart and put it back together as four lines you can write before anything is drawn: who looks, what they ask and when, what they decide, and how long the glance lasts. Then we sort out what a dashboard is not, because half the "dashboards" in any company are reports or analyses wearing the wrong clothes.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Saltmarsh is an invented home-goods retailer that sells through marketplaces and ships from one warehouse. Priya, the managing director, has asked for "a dashboard". Tomas, the analyst, has to design it, and the person who will actually open it is Nia, the operations lead, at the Monday 09:00 stand-up with Kofi from the warehouse. Everything Tomas does across the six sessions is a move you can make on a screen of your own. Where a Saltmarsh card shows numbers, the numbers are real daily aggregates from the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, and the page says so each time.

Part 1 · covers Few's definition, read at source

A screen, a question, a schedule

5 min live

In a 2004 article called "Dashboard Confusion", Stephen Few wrote the sentence most of the field still quotes: "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance." Four things carry the weight in that sentence, and they are the four lines of tonight's brief: an objective someone holds, information chosen for it, one screen, and a glance that recurs.

LiveFour words doing the work: objectives, important, single screen, glance3 min▶

Objectives means somebody's. Few wrote "needed to achieve one or more objectives", and an objective belongs to a person or a role; a dashboard for "the business" has no one to ask what matters. At Saltmarsh (constructed) the objective is Nia's: deliveries that land when promised, this week. Most important is a selection, which means most of the available information is left off. Few says the same thing from the other side in the same article: the information "must be tailored specifically to the requirements of a given person, group, or function".

A single screen is a hard constraint, not a style. Few is explicit that "the means is a single-screen display"; the moment Nia scrolls, the glance is over and she is reading. Monitored at a glance is the recurring part: monitored means again and again, and a glance means seconds. A screen built for a one-off deep look is a different object with a different name, which is Part 2.

Self-studyWhat the definition does not say, and why that matters3 min▶

Few's sentence names no chart type, no colour, no tool and no "KPI". In the same article he goes out of his way to say a dashboard "is a type of display or style of presentation, not a specific type of information or technology". That cuts both ways. It frees you from the vendor's template: a dashboard can be a printed page on a wall or a spreadsheet tab if it meets the four conditions. It also removes the excuse that a screen full of the tool's default widgets is a dashboard because the tool calls it one.

His later book, Information Dashboard Design, develops the definition and a long list of common pitfalls (reported here; the book is not read at source on these pages, and the Business Intelligence course owns his pitfall library). The one sentence from 2004 is enough for tonight, because the four lines of the brief fall straight out of it.

Real world

"Can we get a dashboard for that?" A familiar pattern in any organisation: a senior person asks for a dashboard, an analyst connects the tool to the warehouse, and the screen fills with every column that was easy to chart. It is admired on launch day and never opened again, because nobody opening it on a Monday has a question it answers in the time they have. Nothing in that sequence is lazy. The brief was skipped, and the tool filled the silence.

Part 2 · covers report, dashboard, analysis

Three jobs that get called by one name

5 min live

Half of what gets called a dashboard is a report: a complete record of a period, read through once, filed. Some of it is an analysis: one question, investigated once, with a conclusion. A dashboard is neither. It is monitored, which means the same screen is opened again and again, and the reader already knows where to look. Sorting the three jobs is the first thing Tomas does for Priya, because each job wants a different artefact and only one of them is a single screen.

LiveThe ten-second sort3 min▶

Ask three questions of any screen someone calls a dashboard. Is it opened on a schedule by the same person? If it is opened once, it is an analysis or a report. Does the reader already know where to look? If every visit starts with a search for the relevant number, it is a report with charts. Does it fit the glance? If answering the question needs a scroll, a filter and a download, it is an analysis tool.

At Saltmarsh (constructed), Priya's request turns out to be three artefacts. Finance wants the monthly reconciliation of carrier invoices against shipments: complete, filed, a report. Priya herself wants to know why late deliveries doubled last March: one question, once, an analysis Tomas will do in a notebook. Nia wants to know every Monday whether deliveries are slipping, demand fell, money fell and where the backlog sits: four recurring questions, one reader, one screen. Only the third is the dashboard, and it is the only one Tomas designs tonight.

Self-studyWhy the mix-up costs more than a name3 min▶

A report built as a dashboard loses its completeness: the single-screen constraint forces cuts a report must not make, so the finance team ends up exporting the data behind it and rebuilding the full table by hand. An analysis built as a dashboard loses its conclusion: a screen of filters invites the reader to redo the investigation every time instead of reading the answer once. A dashboard built as a report loses the glance: Nia scrolls, searches, and after three Mondays stops opening it.

The costs are different, which is why the sort comes before the design. The Business Intelligence course teaches how a leader reads a dashboard and how to commission one; those two pages are linked from the cheat sheet rather than repeated here. Tonight's job is narrower: deciding that the thing is a dashboard at all, and for whom.

Say the sort out loud to the person who asked. "You have asked for three things: a monthly report for finance, one investigation into March, and a Monday screen for Nia. I will build the screen first, because it is the one that gets opened fifty times a year." A sponsor who hears that usually agrees, and has just told you who the user is.

Part 3 · covers the glance and the decision test

The glance it has to survive, and the decision it has to change

5 min live

A glance is not a metaphor. It is a measurable slice of someone's Monday: the time between opening the screen and the first thing they say about it. Design for the slice you actually get, and tie every tile to a decision that slice could change. A tile whose answer changes no decision is information, and information that changes nothing is what a report is for.

LiveMeasure the glance before you design for it3 min▶

Tomas does not guess how long Nia's glance is. He sits in one Monday stand-up with a stopwatch and notes when the screen opens and when the first operational sentence is spoken. At Saltmarsh (constructed) the honest answer is about half a minute before Kofi starts talking about Thursday's backlog, and that half minute is the design budget. Everything the screen has to say about the four questions must be sayable in that time, by Nia, without touching the mouse.

Thirty seconds sounds brutal and is in fact generous. It is long enough to read four large numbers, their direction since last week and their colour, if the layout puts them where the eye lands first, which is session 2. It is not long enough to find a number in a table, change a date filter or read a legend. Session 5 turns this into a repeatable test with five readers and three tasks; tonight the point is only that the glance is a number you can measure, not a word in a definition.

Self-studyThe decision column, and the tiles it kills3 min▶

Every candidate tile gets a row with two columns: the question it answers, and the decision that answer changes, with the name of the person who makes it. "Average basket size by marketplace" is a real, useful number at Saltmarsh (constructed) and it has an empty decision column on Monday morning: nobody reprices anything in the stand-up. It leaves the screen and goes into the monthly report, where someone with a repricing decision will read it. The tile is not wrong. It was on the wrong artefact.

Two things make this harder than it sounds. First, people defend tiles by their interest, not by their decision, and "it is good to know" is the sentence to listen for. Second, a decision can be "do nothing this week", which is a real decision only if the alternative was a real action. "Did the money fall?" earns its row because Priya and Nia use it to decide whether anything else on the screen is worth acting on this week. A row that cannot name a concrete alternative action is a report row.

Real world

The dashboard nobody opens. A familiar pattern: a screen with twenty tiles is launched, usage is high in week one and near zero by month three, and the explanation offered is that people are busy. Ask what decision each tile changed and when. Usually two tiles change a weekly decision and eighteen change nothing, so the two readers who needed the two tiles stopped paying the cost of finding them among the eighteen. Busy people are not the problem. Tiles without decisions are.

Build-along

The four-line brief, and the tile list it produces

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas writes four lines before he opens any tool, and then uses them to sort every candidate tile into "on the screen", "one click away" or "somewhere else". Write the same four lines for a dashboard of your own as you go; if you have none in hand, use Saltmarsh (constructed). The brief is short on purpose. Each line is a question the sponsor answers so the design does not have to guess.

Name the person and the moment. Not a department, a person, with the day and time the screen is opened. If you cannot name one, you do not yet have a dashboard, you have a request. Tomas names Nia and the Monday stand-up.

★ Line 1 · who looks, and whenWho: Nia, operations lead (Kofi, warehouse lead, looks over her shoulder) When: Monday 09:00 stand-up, every week, on the meeting-room screen Also: Priya opens it on her phone on Monday evening, read-only Test: can you say the person's name and the clock time? yes

Write the questions the reader already asks. In the reader's words, as questions, not as metric names. Then count them. More than about four recurring questions and you are designing two screens, or a report.

★ Line 2 · the questions, in Nia's wordsQ1 Are deliveries slipping this week? Q2 Did demand fall? Q3 Did the money fall? Q4 Where is the backlog sitting in the warehouse? Not asked on Monday: basket size, category mix, review scores (those are Priya's monthly questions: a report)

Tie each question to a decision and a name. This is the line most people skip, and the one that cuts the tile count. Write the concrete action and its alternative. "Do nothing" counts only if the alternative was a real action with a cost.

★ Line 3 · what each answer changes, and whose decision it isQ1 slipping? -> Nia calls the carrier today, or books the second carrier Q2 demand fell? -> Priya pauses or extends the marketplace promotion Q3 money fell? -> Priya + Nia decide whether to act on anything this week Q4 backlog? -> Kofi moves two people from picking to packing, or back Empty decision column = not on this screen.

Say what the glance must answer, in one breath. This is the sentence Nia should be able to say thirty seconds after the screen opens, with no mouse. Write it before any layout; session 2 designs the first glance around it.

★ Line 4 · the glance"Deliveries are fine / slipping, demand is up / down on last week, money is up / down, and the backlog is in picking / packing / clear." One sentence, four slots, about 30 seconds (measured once in a stand-up, to be re-measured with five readers in session 5).

Sort every candidate tile with line 3. List everything the data could show, then put each item in one of three bins. Nothing is deleted; it is placed. The screen bin is small because the glance is small.

★ The sort · 23 candidates into three binsON THE SCREEN (6) late deliveries this week · delivery days vs promise · orders this week · revenue this week · backlog by stage · carrier share of late orders ONE CLICK AWAY (5) late orders by region · by carrier · by weekday · orders by marketplace · backlog by hour SOMEWHERE ELSE (12) basket size, category mix, review scores, payment types, freight cost per order ... -> Priya's monthly report, or the March analysis, or nowhere Your numbers will differ. Saltmarsh's are constructed.

Read the brief back to the sponsor. Four lines and three bins, out loud, to Priya. The test question is the one that saves months: "If Nia reads this screen and calls the carrier, will you stand behind that call?"

★ The read-back"Nia opens this at the Monday stand-up. In thirty seconds it answers whether deliveries slipped, demand fell, money fell and where the backlog is. Each answer changes one named decision. Twelve things you asked about are in the monthly report instead. If Nia acts on this screen, will you back her?" A sponsor who hesitates has just told you who the real reader is.

Keep the three bins visible to the team. When someone asks for "one more tile", point at the bins and ask which bin, and what decision moves it to the screen. Most requests sort themselves into "one click away" without an argument, and the glance stays intact.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Sort three screens you already have. For each thing your team calls a dashboard, run the ten-second sort: opened on a schedule by the same person, reader knows where to look, fits the glance. Name which of the three jobs each one is really doing.
  2. Finish your brief. All four lines for a real screen you will build or own. If line 3 is empty for most of the tiles, that is the finding, and it is a useful one.
  3. Time one glance. Sit in one recurring meeting where a screen is opened and note the seconds from open to the first operational sentence. Bring the number.
  4. Bring the line-4 sentence from your brief. Session 2 lays out the first glance around it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Finance asks Tomas for "a dashboard" of every carrier invoice against every shipment for the month, which they read line by line and file. What is it?

Few's definition is about how a display is used, not what tool made it. A complete record read through once and filed is a report; squeezing it onto one screen would cost the completeness a report exists for. The tool and the refresh schedule do not change the job.

2 · A tile shows average basket size by marketplace. It is accurate, interesting, and nobody at the Monday stand-up changes anything because of it. What does the brief say?

Line 3 is the sorter. The tile is not wrong; it is on the wrong artefact. Making it smaller still spends part of a thirty-second glance on something that changes no Monday decision, and a chart of it spends more.

3 · What does "monitored at a glance" commit you to, in Few's definition as read?

Monitored means recurring; a glance means seconds; a single screen means no scroll. Few says in the same article that a dashboard is a style of presentation, not a technology, so neither gauges, real time nor a vendor's object type are part of the definition.

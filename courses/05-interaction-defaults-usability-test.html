Interaction, defaults and the usability test

A dashboard with filters is really many dashboards, and the one that loads first is the one most readers ever see. Tonight is about that default, about the one click of drill a Monday glance can afford, and about finding out, rather than hoping, whether the screen works. Jakob Nielsen's 2000 page on testing with five users is read at source: the formula, the typical 31 percent behind the famous 85, the caveat about comparable users, and the people who disagreed. Then "it gets used" stops being a feeling and becomes three numbers Tomas can count without a survey.

Part 1 · covers filters, drill and defaults

The default is the dashboard most people see

5 min live

Every filter multiplies the screen. A date picker, a carrier selector and a region selector on Saltmarsh's screen (constructed) make hundreds of possible dashboards, and Nia will open exactly one of them on Monday: the one the filters show before she touches anything. So the default is not a technical setting left to the tool. It is the dashboard. It must answer the four stand-up questions on load, for this week, for everything, and it must reset to that every time the screen opens, however the last person left it.

LiveWrite the default as a sentence, then make every filter reset to it3 min▶

The default of a filter is a design decision with a reader attached. Tomas writes it as one sentence for the Saltmarsh screen (constructed): "On load, every card shows this week against last week, for all carriers and all regions, with the week starting Monday." That sentence is the line-4 glance from session 1 turned into filter settings. If the stand-up asks about this week, the date default is this week; if it asks about everything, nothing is pre-selected. A default that shows "last 30 days" because the tool shipped that way answers a question nobody at 09:00 asked.

The second half is reset on load. Many tools remember the last state of a filter per user, or worse, per shared link. That is useful for the analyst who comes back to the same cut every afternoon and dangerous for the Monday reader, who will read whatever Friday's investigation left behind as if it were the week's answer. The rule is simple: the shared screen resets to the default every time it opens, and anyone who wants a remembered cut saves it under their own name, one click away. Nia should never have to check the filter bar to know what she is reading.

Self-studyDrill is one click down and one click back, and it leaves the glance alone3 min▶

Drill is the move from a card to the breakdown behind it: late deliveries this week, click, late deliveries by carrier. Session 3 put those breakdowns in the "one click away" bin, and the interaction rule follows from the bin's name. One click from any card reaches its breakdown; one click returns to the screen exactly as it was. Two clicks is an analysis tool. A drill that changes the filter for the whole screen on the way down, so that returning shows a different dashboard from the one Nia left, breaks the glance for the rest of the meeting, and it is the most common way a well-laid-out screen becomes confusing in use.

Three habits keep drill cheap. The breakdown opens on top of the screen or beside it, never in place of it, so the context stays in view. The breakdown is pre-filtered to the card's own question and nothing else; a carrier breakdown of late orders does not also need a region selector. And the card says it can be drilled, with a visible affordance, because an interaction nobody can see is one nobody will find, which is exactly what the five readers will show you in Part 2. How the drill is built in a given tool belongs next door: Business Intelligence a4, commissioning dashboards.

Real world

The Monday that read the wrong month. A familiar pattern: a leadership screen remembers the last filter state per user. An analyst spends Friday afternoon on one region's returns, closes the laptop, and the shared meeting-room login opens on Monday showing that region, that month. Twenty minutes of a leadership meeting are spent arguing about a collapse in revenue that is one region's seasonal dip, until someone looks at the filter bar. Nothing on the screen was wrong. The default was.

Part 2 · covers the five-user task test, Nielsen 2000 read

Five readers, three tasks, one afternoon

5 min live

The claim everyone has heard is "test with five users and you find 85 percent of the problems". It comes from a page Jakob Nielsen published on 18 March 2000, and it is worth reading at source, because the page says more than the slogan. It gives a model, states the number the model needs, and attaches a caveat that applies directly to a dashboard with two kinds of reader. The method it describes is cheap enough that there is no excuse for skipping it: five people, three tasks, a stopwatch, an afternoon.

LiveWhat the 2000 page actually says: the model, the 31, the caveat3 min▶

Nielsen's page gives a model, not a law: the number of usability problems found with n users is N (1 - (1 - L)^n), where N is the total number of problems in the design and L is the share of them a single user finds. He reports a "typical" L of 31 percent across the projects NN/g had studied, and with that L five users "found 85% of the usability problems". The 85 is not a constant of nature; it is what the curve gives at n = 5 when L is 31 percent. If your readers each find fewer of the problems, five of them find less. The page is also blunt about the other end: "zero users give zero insights".

Two more lines matter for a dashboard. First, the caveat, verbatim: "The formula only holds for comparable users who will be using the site in fairly similar ways". A Saltmarsh screen (constructed) read by operations people at a stand-up and by Priya on her phone on Monday evening has two reader groups, and the page says to test 3 to 4 users from each of two groups, or 3 per group with three or more groups, and never fewer than 3, because one user may be unrepresentative. Second, the page argues for iteration: "Spend this budget on 3 studies with 5 users each!", because the second round tests the fixes and catches what the first design hid. The build-along follows that advice literally.

Self-studyThe disagreement, reported, and what to do with it3 min▶

Not everyone accepted the five-user claim. Spool and Schroeder (2001) argued, in a CHI extended abstract titled "Testing Web Sites: Five Users Is Nowhere Near Enough", that on large e-commerce sites five users found far fewer of the problems than the model promised. That paper is reported here, not read at source, so the right way to carry it is as a named disagreement: L varies by project and by task, and a site with thousands of pages and many paths has a lower L than a single screen with six cards and one click of drill. Nielsen's own page points to later NN/g advice that quantitative studies need around 20 users and card sorting about 15 (reported via that pointer, not read), which is a reminder that five is a number for finding problems, not for measuring how big they are.

For a dashboard the honest position is this. One screen, three tasks and five comparable readers is close to the case where Nielsen's model is strongest, so a first round will find most of what is badly wrong. It will not tell you how many seconds the glance takes on average across the company, and it will not find problems in parts of the screen the three tasks never touch. Choose the tasks to cover the four stand-up questions, run three rounds, and treat the 85 as a reason to start, not a result to report. The wider method, from recruiting to think-aloud protocol, belongs to Product Design; the one test on one dashboard is all that is run here.

Say "comparable" out loud before you pick the five. If the people you can get on Thursday are three operations readers and two finance readers, you have two groups, and Nielsen's page asks for 3 to 4 from each. Either find one more of each or report the two groups separately. A test whose readers are not comparable is still worth running; it just cannot borrow the 85.

Part 3 · covers what "gets used" measures

Used is a number, not a feeling

5 min live

A month after launch Priya will ask whether the dashboard is being used, and the usual answer is a shrug or a page-view count from the tool. Neither is the question. A dashboard is used when the same reader opens it on the schedule, answers the question in the glance, and stops asking the question some other way. Each of those is a thing Tomas can count without a survey, and each one fails in a different direction that points at a different fix.

LiveOpens, seconds, emails: what each one says when it moves3 min▶

Opens are counted per named reader on the named day, because the dashboard was designed for Nia on Monday and not for "the business". Launch week is not a week: everybody opens a new screen once. The number to watch is the fourth Monday. If Nia still opens it, the schedule holds; if she does not, nothing else on this list matters, and the first question is whether the default still answers her four questions. Seconds to the first decision is the glance from session 1, re-measured now that the screen exists: a stopwatch from screen open to the first operational sentence in the stand-up, five Mondays, take the median. It should fall as the hierarchy of session 2 does its work; if it rises, something moved or a filter is not resetting.

Questions still asked by email is the one most teams forget and the most telling. Every message that asks "are we late on carrier B this week?" after the screen exists is a question the screen failed to answer in the moment it was asked. Tomas keeps a plain list for four weeks at Saltmarsh (constructed): who asked, what, and whether the screen could have answered it. The list sorts itself into three piles. Could have answered, reader did not find it: a usability problem for the next round of five. Could not answer, should: a card for the "one click away" bin. Could not answer, should not: a report, which is where the question goes.

Self-studyWhy page views lie, and the number that would make Priya happy for the wrong reason3 min▶

Tool analytics count views: every open by anyone, including the analyst checking the refresh, the sponsor showing it to a visitor, and the browser tab that reloads on its own. A high number is easy to produce and says nothing about whether a decision changed. Time-on-page is worse for a dashboard, because the ideal time on a monitoring screen is short: a reader who stays ten minutes is lost, not engaged. The three numbers above are deliberately tied to the brief from session 1: the named reader, the scheduled moment, the question. Anything that cannot be tied back to a line of the brief is a vanity measure.

There is one more number, and it needs a warning. If the screen is good, the stand-up gets shorter, because the first five minutes of "where are we" disappear. That is a real outcome and Priya will like it. Resist the urge to put it on a slide as "the dashboard saved X minutes a week" with a number you did not measure in the same way before and after. The honest version is the stopwatch median from this Part, before and after, with the dates it was taken. Narrative around a number like that belongs to Visual Storytelling; the number itself is a measurement or it is nothing.

Real world

The screen that was "used" and the inbox that said otherwise. A familiar pattern: a weekly screen reports hundreds of views a month and is declared a success. The analyst who built it still gets the same three questions by message every Monday from the same two people, and answers them by hand from a spreadsheet. The views are real and the questions are real; only one of them is about the dashboard. Count the questions, and the screen's actual job is in front of you.

Build-along

Tomas runs five readers through three tasks

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas books one afternoon, five readers and a meeting room with the screen on the wall. Each reader gets the same three tasks, one at a time, in the same words, and Tomas writes down one outcome per task: done, with the seconds, or stuck, with what they were stuck on. Write the same worksheets for a screen of your own as you go; if you have none in hand, use Saltmarsh (constructed). Every cell on these sheets is constructed to show the shape of the method; your cells will be the first real numbers your screen has ever had.

Write three tasks in the reader's words, one per stand-up question. A task is something the reader does on the screen with a checkable answer, not "look at the dashboard". Pick the three questions whose answers change the biggest decisions. Keep the words identical for every reader.

★ Sheet 1 · the three tasksT1 "It is Monday. Are deliveries slipping this week, yes or no?" done when: the reader says yes or no and points at the card T2 "Where is the backlog sitting right now, picking or packing?" done when: the reader names the stage T3 "Which carrier is behind the late orders this week?" done when: the reader names a carrier (this one needs the drill) Same words every time. Do not help. Do not explain the screen first.

Pick five comparable readers, and say why they are comparable. Nielsen's formula holds for comparable users doing similar things. Name the group each reader belongs to; if you have two groups, you need 3 to 4 from each, which is a second afternoon, not a smaller test.

★ Sheet 2 · the five readersU1 Nia operations, Monday stand-up reader U2 Kofi operations, warehouse, reads over Nia's shoulder U3 Priya managing director, reads it Monday evening on a phone U4 shift lead operations, deputises for Nia on leave U5 finance reads it Monday evening for the money question Comparable? U1, U2, U4 are one group (stand-up, big screen). U3, U5 are an evening, phone-sized group: 2 is under Nielsen's 3 to 4 per group. Report them separately, or find one more evening reader before claiming the 85.

Run each reader alone, one task at a time, with a stopwatch and silence. Start the watch when you finish reading the task; stop it when the reader gives the checkable answer. If they are silent for a while, ask "what are you looking for?" and write the answer down. If they have not got there after a minute, that is "stuck", and the reason they give is the data.

★ Sheet 3 · the outcome grid (Saltmarsh, constructed) T1 slipping? T2 backlog? T3 which carrier? U1 Nia done 9 s done 14 s STUCK looked for a drill, none visible U2 Kofi done 11 s STUCK "backlog STUCK clicked the card, nothing opened by stage"? U3 Priya done 20 s done 18 s done 41 s (found the drill by accident) U4 shift done 15 s STUCK same label done 33 s U5 finance STUCK default done 22 s STUCK same as U1 showed last month One row per reader, one cell per task. Write what they said, not what you think.

Turn the grid into problems by reading the columns. A problem is a reason that appears in more than one cell, or once with a reader who cannot be dismissed. Rank by how many readers it stopped, then by which task it blocked. Three of five stuck on the drill outranks one of five stuck on the default, and both are fixed before anything cosmetic.

★ Sheet 4 · problems found, rankedP1 drill to carrier is hidden 3 of 5 stuck (U1, U2, U5) blocks T3 fix: a visible "by carrier" affordance on the late-deliveries card P2 "backlog by stage" label unclear 2 of 5 stuck (U2, U4) blocks T2 fix: label the card "backlog: picking / packing", show both numbers P3 default showed last month 1 of 5 stuck (U5) blocks T1 fix: reset every filter to this week on load, for every reader Nielsen's model with L = 0.31: five readers find about 85% of what is there. Saltmarsh's L is unknown. So: three fixes, then round two, not a victory lap.

Fix, then run round two with five new readers on the changed screen. The second round has two jobs: confirm that each fix works for people who never saw the old version, and find what the old problems were hiding. A reader who could not find the drill never got far enough to find that the breakdown opens in place of the screen.

★ Sheet 5 · round two, five new readers (constructed)P1 drill 0 of 5 stuck fixed P2 label 0 of 5 stuck fixed P3 default 0 of 5 stuck fixed NEW P4 2 of 5 lost the screen after drilling: the breakdown replaced the dashboard and "back" reset the filters fix: open the breakdown beside the screen; one click back, same screen Round three: same tasks, five readers, 0 stuck -> ship it to Monday.

Start the three counts the Monday it ships. The test says the screen can be used; the counts say whether it is. Set them up before launch so week one is on the record, then read them on the fourth Monday, not the first.

Emails steady -> sort them: usability problem / missing card / report.

Do not sit next to the reader. Sit behind, out of eyeline, and keep the stopwatch under the table. A reader who can see your face will look at it for hints, and the seconds you record will be the seconds it took to read you, not the screen.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Write the default sentence for a screen you own. "On load, every card shows ... for ..., and every filter resets to that." Then open the screen on a different login and check whether it does. Note what it showed instead.
  2. Write three tasks, in the reader's words, for the three questions that change the biggest decisions on that screen. Each must have a checkable answer. Keep them for the next item.
  3. Run the test with at least three readers. Five if you can get them; Nielsen's page says never fewer than three. Fill the outcome grid, read the columns, and write the ranked problems. Bring the grid, including the cells that embarrass you.
  4. Start the usage ledger on one screen that is already live: opens by the named reader, seconds to the first decision in one meeting, questions still asked by message this week. Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The Saltmarsh screen remembers each user's last filter state. Tomas drills into carrier B for one region on Friday; the shared meeting-room login opens on Monday. What does Nia see, and what is the rule?

The default is the dashboard most readers see, and a remembered filter makes the last visitor's investigation the week's truth for the next one. Nobody at 09:00 reads the filter bar first, and a prompt on open spends the glance on a question the brief already answered.

2 · A colleague says "five users find 85 percent of problems, Nielsen proved it, so our five readers found 85 percent". Which reply matches what the 2000 page actually says, as read?

The page gives a model and a typical L, with a verbatim caveat about comparable users, and argues for three rounds of five rather than one round of fifteen. The 20 figure is a pointer for quantitative studies, not a withdrawal; 31 percent is one user's share, not five users'. The disagreement is real and is carried as reported.

3 · A month after launch the tool reports hundreds of views, and the analyst still answers "are we late on carrier B?" by message every Monday from the same two people. Is the dashboard used?

Views count anyone, including the analyst and the refresh. Opens by the named reader are necessary, not sufficient. The messages are the measurement: this one is probably a hidden drill (a usability problem) or a "one click away" breakdown, and only the sort says which; putting it on the main screen for everyone spends the glance on a question two people ask.

Learn Dashboard Design with Phoebe · Session 4 of 6

Colour as state

A red tile is a sentence. It says "this is wrong, act", and the reader believes it in the half second before any number is read. That is why colour on a dashboard is the most powerful thing you control and the most abused: tools paint tiles by category, by brand, by whatever hue was next in the default list, and the reader's eye cannot tell a painted red from a meant one. Tonight we take the KPI card apart into its three pieces, give red, amber and green one job only, which is to state the card's condition, and then make sure that state survives a reader who cannot tell red from green, a projector that washes out amber, and a printout in black and white. Where the card shows numbers, they are Olist's; where they are Saltmarsh's, they are constructed, and the page says which.

Part 1 · covers the KPI card anatomy

Value, delta, context band: three pieces, three questions

5 min live

A KPI card looks like a big number in a box, and most are built as exactly that. A big number alone answers only "what is it", and Nia's question on Monday is never that; it is "is it worse, and worse than what". So the card carries three pieces, each answering one question. The value says what it is now. The delta says which way it moved and by how much since the last glance. The context band says what normal has looked like lately, so the reader can see whether the value sits inside normal or outside it. Colour, when it arrives in Part 2, is a summary of that third piece, and nothing else.

LiveWhy the big number is the least informative piece3 min▶

Put 4.9 percent on a card and hand it to someone who has never seen the series. They cannot tell whether that is a good week. Olist's daily late share, the series Saltmarsh's card borrows, has a median of 4.9 percent across 581 days and a worst day of 32.1 percent on 2 March 2018, with a mean of 2.7 percent over its first 28 days and 6.7 percent over the whole span. The same 4.9 is a bad week against the early data and an ordinary one against the whole. The value on its own cannot say which, and Nia's glance has no time to work it out.

The delta fixes half of that: up 0.6 points on last week is a direction and a size, and a direction is what a decision hangs on. The context band fixes the other half: a shaded stripe showing where the last several weeks have sat, so this week's dot is visibly inside it or outside it. The band is drawn from the series, not from a target; a target is a wish, and a card that colours against a wish goes red for a year while the business grows into it. Session 6 measures exactly that mistake. Tonight the band is simply the third piece of the card, and the piece colour is going to summarise.

Self-studyDelta against what, and the direction of bad3 min▶

Every delta needs a stated comparison, and the choice is a design decision the card must show. Against last week catches a sudden move and misses a slow drift; against the same week last year catches seasonality and misses this month; against the band's centre catches drift and is harder to read. Tomas writes the comparison on the card in words, "on last week", because a delta with an unstated base is a number the reader has to interpret, and interpretation is what the glance cannot afford.

The second thing the delta needs is a direction of bad. For late deliveries, up is bad; for orders and revenue, down is bad; for delivery days against the promise, a larger number is worse. A card that shows an upward arrow in the same style on every KPI makes the reader translate on every tile. Tomas orients each card so that the bad direction reads the same way across the screen, and writes the orientation into the card's definition sheet, because that same orientation is what the colour rule in Part 2 is going to use. This is also the point where "colour theory" ends and "card design" begins: how hues are built and which chart forms suit which data belong to the Dataviz course, https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-dataviz-with-phoebe/courses/04-colour-that-works.html; what a card says and when it is allowed to say it is ours.

Real world

The card that was always "up". A familiar pattern: a revenue card shows a green upward arrow every Monday because it compares this week with the same week three years ago, when the company was a third of the size. Everyone has stopped looking at the arrow, so the one Monday revenue actually fell on the week before, the arrow was still green and still up. Nothing on the card was false. The delta's base was never written down, and a delta nobody can read is decoration.

Part 2 · covers colour as state, WCAG 2.2 1.4.1, 1.4.3 and 1.4.11, read

Red, amber, green have one job, and colour is never the only channel

5 min live

On a dashboard, red, amber and green are reserved words. They mean, and only mean, the state of a card: outside the band on the bad side (red), near the edge of it (amber), inside it (green). They are never a category, never a brand, never a highlight for the card someone finds interesting, and never the colour of a chart line that happens to be about something negative. The reason is the reader's reflex: a red anywhere on the screen triggers "act" before any number is read, and a red that did not mean "act" spends that reflex. Spend it twice and the reader stops reacting to red at all, which is the one failure a monitoring screen cannot survive.

LiveThe reserved-word rule, and the three states3 min▶

Tomas writes one sentence on the Saltmarsh (constructed) design sheet and reads it to Priya: "Red, amber and green on this screen mean one thing, where this week sits against the band, and nothing else gets those three colours." Every other colour on the screen, the chart lines, the group headers, the one accent, comes from the palette, which is ink blue with a signal green used only as a fill under dark text. That separation is physical: the three state colours are their own tokens in the stylesheet, and nothing in the palette is allowed to look like them. A chart line is never red because it is about a problem. A header is never green because the team hit a target last quarter.

Green means inside the band: normal, no action. Amber means near its edge: watch, ask a question. Red means outside the band on the bad side: act, this week. Where exactly the edges sit, and how many days a value must stay outside before the card changes, are the two decisions session 6 scores against 581 real days; tonight's rule is only that there are three states, that every one of them is earned by the band and not by a feeling, and that a card with no band cannot have a colour at all. That last clause is Part 3.

Self-studyWCAG 2.2 on a card: 1.4.1, 1.4.3 and 1.4.11, read at source4 min▶

Three criteria of the W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2 (the 12 December 2024 Recommendation) decide whether a state can be read. 1.4.1 Use of Color, Level A: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." A red card with nothing else saying red fails it. So the state is carried three ways on Tomas's card: the colour, the word ("RED", "AMBER", "GREEN") printed in the corner, and the position of this week's dot against the band. Any one of the three can go missing, on a projector, in a printout, in a reader's eyes, and the other two still state it.

1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA: text has "a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1", large-scale text "at least 3:1", where large scale is "at least 18 point or 14 point bold". The value on a card is large; the delta and the word are not, so they need 4.5:1. That is why the taught state colours are a pair each: a strong tone for the border and the dot, and a pale tint for the fill, with dark ink text on the tint. White text sits only on the strong red. 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, Level AA: graphical objects needed to understand content "have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)". The band, the dot and the card border are graphical objects, so a dot has to be visible against the band it sits on, not only distinguishable by hue. 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), Level AAA, raises the text floor to 7:1 and is worth meeting on the one card the reader looks at first.

Checked with the WCAG relative-luminance formula on this page's own tokens, so you can re-check with any contrast checker: ink on each of the three tints clears 14:1; white on the strong red is 6.57:1; white on the strong amber is 2.17:1 and on the strong green 3.45:1, which is why neither carries small text; and the strong red against the strong green is 1.91:1, which means a red dot on a green band is not a visible dot by 1.4.11, whatever the reader's eyes can do. The white-on-primary figure for the palette, 5.74:1, is recorded in the source map.

Say the rule in the first line of the screen's definition sheet. "Red, amber, green: card state only, from the band, with the word printed." A team that has read that sentence stops asking for the returns tile to be red because returns are bad, and starts asking the right question instead: what band, and who draws it.

Part 3 · covers colour vision deficiency and the cards where colour means nothing

Strip the colour off and see what is left

5 min live

The National Eye Institute's page on colour blindness says "About 1 in 12 men have color vision deficiency", and that "The most common type of color vision deficiency makes it hard to tell the difference between red and green." The page gives no figure for women, so none is given here. In a Monday stand-up of twelve people, the odds are that someone is reading a red-amber-green card without the red and the green. The test for that is not a simulator; it is a question you can ask of any card by covering its colour with your hand: what is left, and does it still say the state?

LiveRed-green is the common case, and the second channel is the fix3 min▶

The NEI figure is the only prevalence number worth carrying into a design review, because it is read at source and it is enough: about 1 in 12 men, most commonly a difficulty telling red from green, which is precisely the pair a state card leans on. The response is not to abandon red and green; readers without the deficiency find them instantly, and the convention is understood in every meeting room. The response is the second channel from Part 2, made mandatory: the word printed on the card, and the dot's position against the band. A red-green reader sees two tiles of similar tone, reads "RED" on one and "GREEN" on the other, and sees one dot above its band and one inside. The state arrives. Colour was a shortcut for the people it works for, never the message.

Two cheap habits make this real. Tomas keeps the strong tones for borders and dots, and the pale tints for fills, so even a reader who sees red and green as the same hue sees a light card and a strong border, which is a luminance difference and survives. And he never puts two states next to each other as colour alone: a red dot on a green band fails 1.4.11 at 1.91:1 regardless of eyes, so the band is drawn in the palette's signal green tint and the dot in the state's strong tone, with the word doing the rest.

Self-studyWhen colour should mean nothing, and the Okabe-Ito palette, reported3 min▶

Not every card earns a state. On Saltmarsh's (constructed) screen the backlog card shows where orders are sitting, picking or packing or dispatch, and Kofi moves people on the shape of it, not on a threshold. It has no band, so it has no state, so it has no red, amber or green, ever; it stays ink blue, and that absence is itself information: this card is read, not triaged. The same holds for any card whose question is "which" or "where" rather than "is it worse". A screen where every tile is coloured has told the reader nothing about which tiles are being watched. A screen where two of six carry a state has told her exactly where the rule lives.

Where a chart on the screen needs several hues for categories, the hues come from the palette and never from the three state tokens, and they are chosen for readers with colour vision deficiency. The widely reproduced Okabe and Ito "Color Universal Design" set is the usual starting point: black #000000, orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73, yellow #F0E442, blue #0072B2, vermilion #D55E00, reddish purple #CC79A7. That set is reported here, not read at source, and it is not the palette drawn on these cards; it is named so that you know what to look up when a category chart lands on your screen. Categorical colour, sequential scales and how a hue is built belong to the Dataviz course at https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-dataviz-with-phoebe/.

Real world

The projector that ate amber. A familiar pattern: a screen looks perfect on the analyst's monitor, and on the meeting-room projector every amber card reads as a pale nothing next to the whites. For three weeks the stand-up discusses only the red cards, because amber has physically disappeared from the room. Nobody in that room has a colour deficiency. The card carried its warning as a tint alone, and a tint is the first thing a bad projector removes. A printed word would have survived; so would a dot sitting visibly at the band's edge.

Build-along

The late-deliveries card in four states

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas takes the one card Nia reads first, late deliveries, and draws it four times: green, amber, red, and the state that is no state, a card that carries no colour because it has no band yet. The layout is constructed and the band's edges are placeholders he writes as such; the values on the cards are Olist's, the median daily late share across the whole series and the medians after two of the three changes the session 6 rule finds. Write the same worksheets for a card of your own as you go. If you have none, borrow this one.

Draw the three pieces for your card. Value, delta with its stated base, and the band with its stated window. Write the direction of bad in words. Tomas fills it for late deliveries; the values are Olist's, the rest is constructed.

★ Worksheet 1 · the card anatomyCard: Late deliveries (share of this week's deliveries after the promised date) Value: 4.9% (Olist's median daily late share, whole series) Delta: +0.6 pts base: last week (constructed) Band: last 8 weeks (window stated on the card; width decided in session 6) Bad is: UP (a larger share is worse) Test: can a stranger read all three pieces without asking you anything? yes

Decide whether this card earns a state at all. A card has a state only if it has a band and a direction of bad. Go down your six cards and mark each one. Expect fewer than you think.

Two never will, and the screen says so by staying blue.

Write the three states as sentences, with the action each one triggers. If a state has no action, it is not a state; merge it into its neighbour. The edges are placeholders tonight, written as such.

Shown tonight: 4.9% green, 6.2% amber, 24.3% red (Olist's medians, Tomas's placeholder edges).

Add the second and third channels. The word, printed on the card in the state's strong tone or on its pill; the dot's position against the band. Then cover the colour with your hand and read the card again.

★ Worksheet 4 · the strip testChannel 1 colour pale tint fill + strong border + strong dot (state tokens only) Channel 2 word "GREEN" / "AMBER" / "RED" printed bottom-left Channel 3 position this week's dot drawn against the band Cover the colour: word still there? yes dot still above the band? yes Print in greyscale: word still there? yes dot still above the band? yes Projector: tint washes out; border, word and dot survive A card that fails any row of this test fails WCAG 2.2 1.4.1 (colour as the only means).

Check the contrast of every text and every shape on the card. 4.5:1 for small text, 3:1 for large text and for the band, dot and border against what they sit on. Use any contrast checker; the numbers below are this page's own tokens, computed with the WCAG formula.

★ Worksheet 5 · the contrast floors (WCAG 2.2 1.4.3 and 1.4.11)ink #141C2E on red tint #FDE8E6 14.46 : 1 small text PASS (4.5 needed) ink #141C2E on amber tint #FFF3D1 15.38 : 1 small text PASS ink #141C2E on green tint #DCF5E6 14.77 : 1 small text PASS white on strong red #B42318 6.57 : 1 small text PASS (the only white-on-state pair) white on strong amber #E8A317 2.17 : 1 FAIL for any text: ink only on amber white on strong green #1E9E5A 3.45 : 1 large text or shapes only, never small text strong red dot on strong green band 1.91 : 1 FAIL 1.4.11: never a state dot on a state band Rule that falls out: tint under ink text, strong tone for border and dot, white only on red.

Read the four cards back to Nia. Four sentences, one per state, out loud. The test is whether she says the action before you do.

★ The read-back"Green: deliveries are where they have been, I move on. Amber: they are at the edge, I ask Kofi what Thursday looked like. Red: they are outside, I call the carrier before lunch. Blue: that card has no line to cross; I read it, I do not triage it." If Nia reaches the action before you finish the sentence, the state is doing its job.

Keep the state tokens in one place and the palette in another. One stylesheet block for red, amber and green, named as states; one for everything else. When a chart line "needs to be red because it is about complaints", the question becomes "which state is it in", and the request answers itself.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Audit the colour on one screen you own. List every red, amber or green on it and write beside each what it means. Any one that means a category, a brand or "important" rather than a state is paint; note what you would replace it with.
  2. Draw your first card in four states on paper, with the value, the delta and its base, the band and its window, the word and the dot. Cover the colour with your hand and check it still reads.
  3. Run the contrast floors on your real tokens with any checker: 4.5:1 for small text, 3:1 for large text and for every band, dot and border against its background. Bring the one pair that failed.
  4. Note which of your six cards carry no state at all, and why. Session 5 puts the whole screen in front of five readers and three tasks, and the first task is "which card would you act on".
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Priya asks for the returns card to be red "because returns are a problem for us". Nothing on the returns card is outside its band this week. What does the reserved-word rule say?

Red, amber and green are reserved for the card's state, earned by the band. A category or a priority is not a state. A compromise colour is still an unearned colour, and the reader cannot tell it from an earned one.

2 · A late-deliveries card shows a pale red fill and nothing else to mark its state. Which WCAG 2.2 criterion does it fail, as read, and what fixes it?

1.4.1 Use of Color: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information". Darkening the red or adding a border changes contrast, not the number of channels. A legend is still colour as the only channel on the card itself.

3 · The backlog card shows where orders sit in the warehouse, picking, packing or dispatch, and Kofi acts on its shape. Should it carry a red, amber or green?

A state needs a band and a direction of bad. "Large" without a band is a feeling, and one colour per stage is a category wearing state colours. A screen where only the cards with a rule carry a colour tells the reader exactly where the rule lives.

Learn Dashboard Design with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Density, grouping and white space

Session 2 decided where the eye lands first. Tonight is about everything else on the screen: how much of it there is, how it is grouped, and where the rest goes. The pressure is always in one direction. Every stakeholder has one more tile that is "good to know", the tool makes adding it free, and the screen fills until the glance is gone. Three ideas push back: one screen is a wall and not a style, tiles are grouped by the question the reader is asking and not by the system the data came from, and a number that is not on the screen is not deleted, it is one click away or in a report. Then Tomas takes the 23 candidate tiles from session 1 and cuts them to six, with a worksheet you can run on your own pile.

Part 1 · covers one screen and the ink on it

The fold is where the glance ends

5 min live

Few's 2004 definition, read at source, says the information is "consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance", and in the same article: "The means is a single-screen display." That is not a preference for tidy layouts. It is a mechanical fact about reading. The moment Nia scrolls, she is no longer glancing at a screen; she is reading a page, and the tiles below the fold are read in a different mode by a different part of her attention, if they are read at all. Density is therefore a budget question before it is a taste question: how much can the single screen carry and still be glanced at?

LiveOne screen is a wall, not a suggestion3 min▶

Read Few's sentence again with the word "single" underlined. "The means is a single-screen display" is the mechanism by which a dashboard is monitored at a glance at all: the eye can take in a fixed screen without a hand on the mouse, and it cannot take in a scrolled one. At Saltmarsh (constructed) the screen is the meeting-room display at the Monday 09:00 stand-up, and Nia does not touch the keyboard during the thirty seconds measured in session 1. Anything that needs a scroll to reach is, for her, not on the dashboard. It may still be a good number. It is in the wrong layer, which is Part 3.

This turns density into arithmetic. The screen has a fixed area; each tile has a minimum size at which its value, its direction and its label are legible from the back of the room; divide one by the other and you have the number of tiles the screen can carry. For a meeting-room display read from three metres that number is small. For a laptop at arm's length it is larger, and the glance is shorter because the reader is alone with it. The budget comes from the room and the reader, never from how many numbers there are.

Self-studyData-ink, reported, and the chrome that creeps in3 min▶

Edward Tufte's data-ink ratio is reported here rather than read at source: the idea, from The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983), is that some of the ink on a chart carries data and the rest carries decoration, framing or repetition, and that the share carrying data should be high. No number is quoted on this page because the book is not read here. The idea is enough. Look at the large tile in the figure: the value, its direction and the eight-week line are the data; the gridlines and ticks frame it; the drop shadow, the logo and the heavy border carry nothing. On a dense screen that chrome is paid for out of the same budget as the tiles, and it is the first thing to erase.

The chrome creeps in because tools add it by default and because each piece looks harmless on its own. A card border, a shadow, a tool logo in the corner, a duplicated title above the tile and inside it, a legend for a single series, axis labels on a sparkline nobody reads at a glance. Six of these on six tiles is thirty-six things that are not data on a screen meant to be read in thirty seconds. Chart form itself, and which marks carry data best, belong to Dataviz: https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-dataviz-with-phoebe/. Tonight's rule is narrower: anything on the screen that is not a value, a direction, a label or a context band must argue for its place.

Real world

The dashboard with a scroll bar. A familiar pattern: the screen was designed on a large monitor by the analyst who built it, opened on a laptop by the people who read it, and the bottom third disappeared below the fold. Nobody mentions it because nobody knows it is there. Months later a leader asks why a number "was never on the dashboard", and it was, forty pixels below the edge. The fix is not a smaller font. It is deciding, tile by tile, what the single screen is for.

Part 2 · covers tiles against tables, and grouping

Group by the question, not by the source

5 min live

Two decisions shape density more than any font size. The first is form: whether a number is a tile (one value, its direction, a label, read in a second) or a row in a table (many values compared across rows, read in a scan). The second is grouping: the same six tiles can be arranged by where the data came from, which is how the tool thinks, or by the question the reader is asking, which is how Nia thinks. The eye reads groups before it reads tiles, so the grouping decides how many things the reader has to hold at once.

LiveTile or table: one number, or many rows3 min▶

A tile is for a number the reader checks: one value, its direction since last time, its label, and (from session 4) its state. It is read in about a second and it answers yes or no. A table is for numbers the reader compares: several rows of the same measure, scanned to find the biggest, the odd one, the one that moved. It is read in a scan, not a glance, and it answers "which one". Two questions decide the form for any candidate: does Nia check it or compare it? and does the answer come from one value or from the differences between several? Check and one value: tile. Compare and several: table.

At Saltmarsh (constructed) "late deliveries this week" is a tile: one share, one direction, checked. "Late orders by carrier" is a comparison across three or four carriers, so it is a table or a short bar list, and it is only needed when the tile is not fine. That already tells you where it lives: not beside the tile competing for the glance, but one click behind it. Tables on the single screen are the commonest density failure, because a table is a grid of numbers that all look equally important, and the glance has nowhere to land.

Self-studyGroup by the question: Miller's chunk, read at source4 min▶

Tools group by data source because that is how the data arrived: the order system, the carrier feed, the marketplace export, the warehouse system. The reader groups by the question in her head. When those two disagree, the reader does the joining work herself every Monday, and in the figure that is the dashed arrow: Nia's "are deliveries slipping" is answered by two tiles under "order system" and one under "carrier feed", and she carries the first two in her head while she finds the third. Group by the question, name each group in her words, and the join disappears. Four headings that are questions also do a second job: they tell a new reader what the screen is for without a manual.

The usual citation for group size is George Miller's 1956 paper, "The magical number seven, plus or minus two", read here at source. It is worth being exact about what it does not say. Miller's seven is the span of absolute judgment for one-dimensional stimuli in laboratory tasks (tones, loudness, line lengths and positions) and the span of immediate memory counted in chunks. The paper says nothing about screens, displays of information, or how many items belong on one; its closing words call the match between the two sevens "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". So "no more than seven tiles, Miller said so" is an extrapolation Miller did not make. What the paper does support is the move he calls recoding: a chunk can carry more if its parts are grouped, which is exactly what a question heading does. Design for four groups the reader can hold, not for a tile count. Miller also notes that adding dimensions raises total capacity while lowering accuracy per attribute, which is a fair warning against tiles that encode three things at once.

Write the group headings as the reader's questions, verbatim. "Are deliveries slipping?" on the screen, not "Logistics KPIs". If a tile does not sit naturally under any of the question headings, that is not a layout problem; it is session 1's decision column saying the tile has no Monday question, and it leaves.

Part 3 · covers progressive disclosure

On the screen, one click away, in a report

5 min live

White space on a dashboard is not emptiness; it is the numbers you chose to put somewhere else. Progressive disclosure is the name for choosing where: the screen answers "is anything wrong?", one click behind it answers "where, and which one?", and a report answers "why, over the month?". Each layer has a different reader at a different moment, and each is designed, not left over. A tile that is cut from the screen is placed in a layer, with a path to it, so that nothing the business needs is deleted and nothing the glance does not need is in its way.

LiveThree layers, three readers, three questions3 min▶

The screen is for "is anything wrong?", and its reader is Nia at the Monday 09:00 stand-up with no mouse in her hand. Everything on it is a check. One click away is for "where, and which one?", and its reader is Nia or Tomas, the same morning, after the glance has found something: the late-deliveries tile is red, so she clicks it and sees late orders by carrier, by region, by weekday. Those views sit behind the tile they explain, so the click is a question and the view is its answer. A report is for "why, over the month?", read once, top to bottom, by Priya, with the twelve things she asked about that have no Monday decision.

Each layer is designed. At Saltmarsh (constructed) the five one-click views are drawn with the same care as the six tiles, because the moment Nia needs them is the moment the dashboard matters most: something is wrong and she has to say what. The report is designed too, as a report, with the completeness a report needs and none of the single-screen constraints. The failure mode is leaving the second and third layers undesigned and then, when someone asks for a number, putting it on the screen because that is the only layer that exists.

Self-studyWhat disclosure must not hide3 min▶

Disclosure has a limit: a tile cannot move off the screen if the glance needs it to answer "is anything wrong?". The test is session 1's line 4, the sentence Nia should be able to say thirty seconds after the screen opens. Every slot in that sentence needs a tile on the screen; everything that is not a slot can move. The second limit is context. A value alone cannot answer "is anything wrong?", so a tile that stays on the screen keeps its direction and its comparison with it (session 4 draws the card anatomy); moving the "against last week" part one click away to save space leaves a number that cannot be checked.

Two things change how the layers behave in practice. The default view of the one-click layer is what most readers see, and a filter that resets on load is a different design from one that remembers; that is session 5. And a leader who reads only the screen is reading a complete answer to one question, not a summary of everything, which is worth saying out loud when the screen is launched. How a leader should read such a screen, and how to commission one, belong next door: https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-business-intelligence-with-phoebe/courses/a2-reading-a-dashboard.html and https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-business-intelligence-with-phoebe/courses/a4-commissioning-dashboards.html.

Real world

"Can you just add it to the dashboard?" A familiar pattern: a request arrives for one more number, the only object anyone can point to is the screen, and so the screen grows by one tile a month until the glance is gone. Teams that keep a second layer have a different conversation: "yes, it goes behind the deliveries tile, one click, here is where". The request is met, the screen is unchanged, and the person asking usually finds the click is exactly where they would have looked.

Build-along

Twenty-three tiles to six, without deleting anything

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas starts from the 23 candidate tiles of session 1 and ends with six on the screen, five behind a click, twelve in a report, arranged under four question headings with room between them. The counts are Saltmarsh's and constructed; yours will differ. Run each worksheet on your own pile as you go. If you have no pile in hand, use Saltmarsh. The worksheets are plain text on purpose: the layout comes out of the answers, and a tool is the last thing opened.

List the pile, one line each. Every candidate tile, in the words it was asked for, with who asked. Do not sort yet. Tomas's pile at Saltmarsh (constructed) has 23 lines; the point of writing them all down is that nobody can later say their tile was forgotten.

Tile or table, for each of the six. Two questions per survivor: does Nia check it or compare it, and does the answer come from one value or from differences between several? A table that survives sieve 2 is a warning: it usually means a comparison is being asked to do a check's job.

★ Worksheet 4 · formtile check / compare one value / several form late deliveries this week check one tile delivery days vs promise check one tile late orders by carrier compare several (3-4 rows) short bar list orders this week check one tile revenue this week check one tile backlog by stage compare several (3 stages) 3 small tiles in a row Rule: one table on the screen at most, and only if the glance needs a "which".

Group by the question, in the reader's words. Assign each survivor to one of the four questions, write the heading as Nia would say it, and count tiles per group. A survivor that fits no question has just failed sieve 1 late, and goes back to worksheet 2.

★ Worksheet 5 · groupsheading (Nia's words) tiles count Are deliveries slipping? late this week · days vs promise · by carrier 3 Did demand fall? orders this week 1 Did the money fall? revenue this week 1 Where is the backlog? backlog by stage 1 Groups to hold: 4. Tiles: 6. Miller 1956 supports the four, not the six. Order of groups = order of the glance sentence (session 2).

The one-screen check, on the real screen. Open the layout on the device and in the room where it will be read, and from the reader's distance. Nothing may need a scroll; every value must be legible from where Nia sits; the space between groups must be wider than the space between tiles inside a group, so the groups read as groups. Then say the glance sentence out loud with no mouse.

★ Worksheet 6 · the one-screen checkdevice and room: meeting-room display, Monday 09:00, Nia at the table scroll needed? no legible from the reader's seat? values yes · labels yes · bar list: enlarge gap between groups > gap inside groups? yes glance sentence said with no mouse? yes, by Nia, not by Tomas Fails any line -> cut chrome first (shadows, logos, duplicate titles), then move a tile one click away. Never shrink the type.

Keep the three counts on the wall: 6 · 5 · 12. When the next request arrives, the answer is a placement, not a yes or no: "it goes behind the deliveries tile" or "it goes in the monthly report". The screen count moves only when a slot in the glance sentence changes, and that is a session 1 conversation with the sponsor, not a layout tweak.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Run the two sieves on your own pile. List every candidate tile for a screen you own, sieve 1 for a decision, sieve 2 for the glance, and write the three counts. If the screen count is above eight, go back to the line-4 sentence and check how many slots it really has.
  2. Regroup one existing dashboard by question. Take a screen that is grouped by source or by team, write the reader's questions as headings, and move the tiles under them on paper. Count how many tiles the reader currently has to join by eye.
  3. Erase the chrome on one tile. Pick one card and list everything on it that is not a value, a direction, a label or a context band. Remove it in the tool or on paper, and note what the tile lost. Usually nothing.
  4. Bring your six. Session 4 draws the anatomy of a single card and gives each of your six a state; bring the list of survivors with their form (tile or table) decided.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A colleague says the screen must have at most seven tiles because "Miller 1956 proved it". What does the paper, read at source, actually support?

Miller's seven is the span of absolute judgment for one-dimensional stimuli and the span of immediate memory in chunks, with no claim about displays of information. The paper's own words call the match "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". The usable idea is recoding: group so that the reader holds four questions, not nineteen tiles.

2 · The tool has laid Nia's six tiles out under four headings: Order system, Carrier feed, Marketplace, Warehouse. Her first question, "are deliveries slipping?", needs tiles from the first two. What is the fix?

The reader groups by the question in her head, and when the screen groups by the system the data came from she does the joining work herself every Monday. Headings that are her questions remove the join and tell a new reader what the screen is for. Removing headings or flattening everything into one row gives up the chunk, which is the one thing the grouping was providing.

3 · "Late orders by carrier" is a comparison across three carriers that Nia needs only when the late-deliveries tile is not fine. Where does it live?

The screen answers "is anything wrong?"; the click answers "where, and which one?". A comparison that is needed only after the check has failed belongs in the second layer, behind its parent tile, designed with the same care. A table on the screen competes with the glance; the report is for "why, over the month", and nothing is deleted.

The red-amber-green bench

A KPI card turns red because a number crossed a line someone drew. Tonight you draw the line. The bench holds 581 real days of orders, revenue and delivery performance from a Brazilian marketplace, shipped as daily aggregates, and a late-deliveries card for Saltmarsh's Monday screen. You set where amber and red begin, and the bench counts what that costs: how many days a month the card goes red or amber with nothing really wrong, how many sustained changes it never flagged, and how much of the year it spent in each colour. Five rungs make the card better. Two buttons make it worse, and the bench prints why.

Part 0

Where we start

Saltmarsh is constructed; its numbers tonight are not. The late-deliveries card on Nia's Monday screen is fed by the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist: 98,639 orders placed between 23 January 2017 and 26 August 2018, reduced to one row per day. Nothing about an order, a customer, a product or a review leaves the source; what the bench holds is a count, a sum and two averages per calendar day, and that is enough to ask the only question that matters about a colour: when this card went red, was anything actually wrong?

Part 1 · covers what a threshold is, and what a real change is

Two lines on a card, and one rule behind the bench

6 min live

Red, amber and green on a KPI card are a function of two numbers: the amber threshold and the red threshold, in the direction that is bad for this KPI. Session 4 built the card; tonight asks whether its colour is worth trusting. To score a colour you need a second thing, independent of the thresholds: a definition of when something was really wrong. The bench states one, as a rule, and scores every colour against it.

LiveWhat the bench measures, and what it merely defines3 min▶

Measured: for the thresholds you set, the colour of the card on each of 525 scored days, the days it was red or amber with no real change (false-alarm days, printed per month), how many separate alarm episodes that made, how many real changes were never flagged within 14 days (misses), how many days the flag took on average, and the share of days in each colour. Those are facts about the series and your thresholds, computed in your browser and agreed to the last digit with an independent Python run.

Defined: what a "real change" is. The bench orients every KPI so that larger is worse, slides two adjacent 28-day windows along the series, and calls a day a real change when the median of the next 28 days sits two robust spreads above the median of the previous 28, at a peak, with no other change within 28 days. Only shifts for the worse count; improvements are not scored; a one-day spike is not a change. That rule is printed on the widget because it is a choice, and you could make a different one. The window-sliding idea is the standard one in the change-point literature (Truong, Oudre and Vayatis, 2020, section 5.2.1); the specific numbers are the bench's.

Self-studyWhere the series comes from, and what was cut3 min▶

Olist is a Brazilian marketplace operator; its public dataset on Kaggle holds about 100,000 orders from 2016 to 2018, anonymised, under a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence, which allows derived, non-commercial, attributed use. The builder script reads the raw tables once and writes four numbers per calendar day: orders placed, the sum of item prices, the mean of delivered date minus estimated date (negative means early), and the share of delivered orders that arrived after the estimate. The raw rows never leave the machine that ran the script.

The dataset's first months and last weeks are sparse, because it was extracted mid-stream. The bench keeps whole weeks from the first Monday whose following seven days all have at least 20 orders to the last such Sunday: 581 days, 83 weeks, 23 January 2017 to 26 August 2018, dropping 141 leading and 52 trailing days. The scoring window is narrower again, days 28 to 552, because the change rule needs 28 days on each side. All of that is printed under the widget, so nobody has to take it on trust.

Real world

"It is always red, so nobody looks." A familiar pattern: a card is set to red below a target chosen in a planning meeting, the business changes, and a year later the card has been red for ten months. Everyone has learned to read past it, which means the week it should have been read, it was not. Nobody chose that; the threshold simply never had a false-alarm count attached to it.

Build-along · the bench

581 real days, your two lines, and the bill

★ 26 min · the main event

The bench opens on the late-deliveries card at rung 4 (a trailing band with persistence), the setting Tomas ends on. Read the card first, then the verdict line, then the five tiles. Work the rungs from 1 upwards and say out loud what moved and what did not before you go on. Move the amber and red sliders at any rung; scrub the day slider to see the card on any Monday. Then press the two buttons that make it worse.

Read the bill in this order. False-alarm days per month first, because that is what Nia pays every week. Misses second, because a miss is the week the card existed for. Red time third: a card red more than a quarter of the time is wallpaper. The numbers quoted in Part 2 are the ones the bench prints at the default sliders, amber at one spread and red at two.

Part 2 · covers the ladder, rung by rung

Five rungs, and what each one buys on the late-deliveries card

5 min live

The bench computes these live; they are written down here so they can be argued with after class. On the late-deliveries card the rule finds three sustained changes for the worse: 8 November 2017, when the median late share went from 4.9 to 11.8 percent; 11 February 2018, from 7.9 to 24.3; and 11 July 2018, from 1.3 to 6.2. Every rung catches all three. They differ entirely in what they cost.

LiveRungs 1 and 2: a number from launch day, and a number from hindsight3 min▶

Rung 1, fixed thresholds. Tomas sets amber and red from the first four weeks of data, median plus one and two spreads, and never touches them again. That is how most thresholds are actually set. On late deliveries the bench prints 13.97 false-alarm days a month and the card red 29 percent of days, because the first four weeks of 2017 happened to be unusually good (2.7 percent late against 6.7 percent over the whole span). Switch the card to delivery-versus-estimate and rung 1 is red 90 percent of the time: the early weeks delivered almost twelve days early, and the thresholds never learned otherwise. Nothing is missed, because a card that is always red misses nothing. It is also never read.

Rung 2, the series' own spread. Thresholds from the median and spread of the whole 581 days: 5.68 false-alarm days a month, red 13 percent. Better, and a cheat. Those thresholds use the whole future of the series; on launch day Tomas has four weeks, and in a growing business the whole-series median is a number he will not see for a year. Rung 2 is on the ladder to show what hindsight is worth, and to make rung 3 honest.

LiveRungs 3 and 4: the band you can run on a Monday, and the two-day wait3 min▶

Rung 3, a context band on the card. Every day, the median and spread of the trailing 28 days; the card draws that band and colours itself against it. This is what session 4's card anatomy was for, and it is the first rung Tomas could actually ship. It is noisier than rung 2: 7.19 false-alarm days a month against 5.68, and 62 separate alarm episodes against 26. The reason is worth saying out loud: a 28-day spread is tighter than the spread of a series that contains its own regime shifts, so a live band draws its lines closer and single bad days cross them. The band also adapts, which cuts both ways: after a sustained shift the new level becomes the new normal within a month, and the card goes quiet again.

Rung 4, persistence. Same band, and the colour changes only when two consecutive days agree. False-alarm days fall to 4.70 a month, episodes to 14, all three changes still caught, now 2.3 days after they start instead of 1.7. That one-day price buys a card whose colour means "this is still true today", which is the only kind Nia can act on. Rung 5, the scorecard, runs rung 4 on all four cards at once; its lesson is in the self-study below.

Self-studyThe scorecard: two cards where every alarm was false3 min▶

On the scorecard rung the orders and revenue cards print zero real changes. The rule looks for sustained shifts for the worse, and in these 83 weeks the business grew: mean daily orders went from 68.8 in the first eight weeks to 223.4 in the last eight, and demand never fell for a sustained month. So every red or amber day those two cards ever showed, 3.30 and 3.07 false-alarm days a month at rung 4, was a false alarm by the rule. The biggest single day in the series, 1,176 orders on 24 November 2017, Black Friday, is a one-day spike and not a change by this rule either; the orders card does not flag it because a spike in orders is good, and a one-day spike in late deliveries would not count as a change even if it were bad.

Two honest readings follow. First, a card with no real changes in its history is a card whose thresholds have never been tested, and the first real fall in demand will meet thresholds tuned on noise. Second, "real change" is a rule, and a different rule, one that counted spikes or improvements, would score the same colours differently. The bench prints its rule so that argument can be had with the numbers in view; the Anomaly Detection course owns the detectors themselves, and Data Observability owns the alerting pipeline the colour would eventually feed.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

Two reflexes that make the card worse, with the bill attached

4 min live

Both buttons are things reasonable people do to a card that has embarrassed them. One follows a miss: tighten the thresholds so nothing gets past. The other follows a month of false alarms: smooth the number until the card stops twitching. The bench lets you do both to the real series and prints what happens.

LiveThe anti-lever: tighten to catch everything2 min▶

Press "Tighten" and the bench runs rung 4 with amber at half a spread and red at one. Misses go from 0 to 0: there was nothing left to catch, because rung 4 already caught all three changes. What moves is the bill. False-alarm days rise from 4.70 to 7.88 a month, two thirds more, and the card is red 29 percent of days against 11. The verdict line says it plainly: a card that is red more than a quarter of the time stops being read, and the week it matters it is read as it always is, which is not at all.

The honest nuance the bench also prints: tightening does flag the three changes sooner, 1.0 days on average against 2.3. If a day of warning were worth 38 extra false-alarm days a year, tightening would be right. On a weekly operations screen it is not, and the arithmetic makes that a decision rather than a taste.

LiveThe break button: hide the variance2 min▶

Press "Hide the variance" and the card shows a 28-day average as its point, draws no band, and colours itself against the whole-series thresholds. It looks like the best card on the page: green 82 percent of days, two alarm episodes in seventeen months. It misses two of the three real changes within 14 days, and on the delivery-versus-estimate card it misses all seven. The reason is arithmetic: a 28-day average can move by at most one twenty-eighth of a shift per day, so a change that doubles the late share needs weeks to drag the point across a line, by which time the stand-up that needed it has passed.

This is why the break button is on the bench. If smoothing made the card look calm and the misses stayed at zero, the bench would be measuring the look of the card and not the operation behind it. The misses climb, so it is measuring the right thing. The card can look calm because the business is calm or because the arithmetic is, and only a false-alarm and miss count tells you which.

Real world

After the miss, the tightening. A familiar sequence: a card stayed green through a bad fortnight, someone senior asked why, and the thresholds were halved the same afternoon. Three months later the card is red most weeks and the same senior person asks why nobody acts on it. Both questions were about the same two numbers, and neither was answered with a count of what the colours cost.

Homework

Before you ship a threshold

30 min
  1. Score one of your own cards. Take a KPI you already colour, write down its amber and red lines and how they were chosen (launch guess, target, spread), and count, over the last three months, the days it was red or amber when nothing was really wrong. Use your own definition of "really wrong", and write that down too.
  2. Run the ladder on the delivery card. Switch the bench to delivery-versus-estimate and read rung 1 (red 90 percent of days). Say in one sentence why launch thresholds did that, then find the rung you would ship.
  3. Find your own two reflexes. Recall one threshold you tightened after a miss and one number you smoothed after a noisy month. What did each cost, in the bench's three terms?
  4. Write the rule on the card. For the KPI card you are shipping, add one line under it that says how its colour is decided (band, persistence, thresholds). If the line is embarrassing, the card is not ready.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the late-deliveries card the bench prints 13.97 false-alarm days a month at rung 1 and 0 of 3 changes missed. What does that pair of numbers tell you?

Misses and false alarms trade against each other; zero misses at a high false-alarm count is the signature of a card that alarms by default. The three changes were caught by every rung, so they are not small, and the bench's Python reference agrees with the browser on every number.

2 · Why is rung 3, the trailing 28-day band, noisier than rung 2, the whole-series spread, on the late-deliveries card (7.19 against 5.68 false-alarm days a month)?

Rung 2's wider spread comes from hindsight: it includes the shifts it is then asked to detect. Rung 3 is the one you can actually run on a Monday, and persistence (rung 4) is how its noise is paid down, not a shorter window.

3 · The "hide the variance" card is green 82 percent of days with two alarm episodes in seventeen months. What has it bought, and what has it cost?

The bench prints the misses precisely so the calm cannot be mistaken for health. A change the reader has to find by looking closely at a line is a change the card did not flag, and the stand-up that needed it has passed.
